# Prologue — three draft treatments to choose from

*Read these as competing directions, not finished pages. Same thesis (**lessons, not lodging**),
three different ways in. Tell me which voice is right — or which pages to splice — and I'll
write the winner into `prologue.json` with thumbnails.*

- **Option A — "Lessons, Not Lodging"** — the flagship. Opens universal (the mirror), lands
  Persian (Sohrab + Zahhak). Balanced, sweeping.
- **Option B — "The Two Snakes"** — Persian-first. Opens *inside* the Shahnameh, then widens.
  Most grounded in your own Mythos.
- **Option C — "A Self-Portrait"** — the cool, psychological pivot. Quietest. Leads with the
  reflection and turns the reader inward fastest.

All three are research-backed (the borrowed lines are marked in the draft notes at the end).

## OPTION A — *Lessons, Not Lodging*

**Tagline:** You did not visit those myths. You recognised them. Here is why the mind keeps
building the same story — and why you must not move in.

### Page 1 — The Reflection

You just walked through a world of gods and heroes, and some of it felt like coming home. Ask
why. A story about kings who never lived, in a language some of you do not speak, about a country
most of you have never seen — why did it reach in and *pull?*

Because it was never a story about them. **It was a mirror, and the face in it was yours.**

Twenty-five centuries ago a man named Xenophanes noticed the trick. *If horses and oxen had
hands,* he said, *horses would draw the gods like horses, and oxen like oxen.* We do the same. We
build makers, and we carve them in our own image — quarrelling, jealous, proud, exactly like the
people doing the carving. The mind is a **myth-making machine.** Show it a silence and it hears a
voice. Show it a mesa on Mars and it sees a face. Show it a world it cannot explain and it hands
you **a who** — a maker, a plan, a reason — because a universe of persons is easy to hold and a
universe of blind accident is not.

So the myths were never out there. They came from **in here.** Which means the interesting subject
was never the gods. It was the machine that keeps making them — the one behind your own forehead.
That machine is what this whole series is about. Welcome down from the myth. Mind the step.

### Page 2 — The Address

Here is the danger, and it is not that the myths are false. It is that they are **comfortable.**

A myth is somewhere you can move into. Inside the story you are the chosen one whose call has not
yet come; the hero whose real life is still ahead; the wronged prince awaiting his hour. Nothing is
asked of you there but to wait, beautifully. And the mind that built the myth will happily hand you
the keys and let you **live in it.**

The Jungians have a name for the person who does. They call it the **provisional life** — the life
lived as a rehearsal, always *for now*, always waiting for the real thing to begin. *"There is
always the secret reservation,"* wrote Marie-Louise von Franz, *"that this is not yet what is really
wanted."* Such a person walks around, she said, *in a cloud of fantasies — full of unlived life.*
The tragedy is not that he refuses to land. **It is that the runway was his life.**

You know the feeling from the inside. It is the grey that settles over real work — the job, the
person, the ordinary Tuesday — the quiet verdict that *this is not it,* that the true story is
elsewhere, later, bigger. That grey is not wisdom. It is the cost of living in the myth. **You have
been admiring the dragon instead of fighting it.**

### Page 3 — Two Ways to Move In

Your own Mythos already shows you exactly how this ends. It shows you twice.

**The first way is to move into the hero.** Rostam is the greatest champion in the world — and so
completely fused with that name that when a young warrior faces him and asks, plainly, *are you
Rostam?*, he lies. He will not take the armour off. He does not know the boy is his son, Sohrab. He
finds out from a token on the body, **after** the blade. The name *Rostam* was a map; the man was
the territory; and **living in the map got the boy killed.** That is what the persona does when you
move in: it swallows the father whole, and you murder the future without ever seeing its face. *Ask
the boy his name before you put the dagger in.*

**The second way is to move into the wound.** Zahhak is kissed on the shoulders by the devil, and
two snakes grow. He does not, exactly, *choose* to become a monster. He agrees to a **feeding
schedule** — the snakes must be fed young men's brains, every day, or they eat him instead. That is
the other lodging: not the glorious self-image, but the injury you build a life around. The snakes
are not a costume. **They are an appetite that now runs the palace.**

One man lodges in the legend. One lodges in the hurt. Notice what they have in common: **both eat
the future.** This is why we visit the myths and do not live in them. *You cannot carry the fire of
the gods without eventually burning down your own house.*

### Page 4 — The Return

So what *is* the myth for, if not to live in? The oldest answer is the best one.

Chesterton said the fairy tale does not invent the dragon — the child already knows the dragon.
**What the fairy tale gives him is a St. George to kill it.** The myth is not a place to hide from
the monster. It is where you go to **borrow the sword,** and then you carry it back out. Tolkien
drew the line exactly: there is the Escape of the *prisoner,* who uses the far-off vision to get
back to the real world and change it — and the Flight of the *deserter,* who uses it to abandon his
post. Same door. Opposite man.

Ferdowsi knew the difference too. Set the champions who lodged against the ones who returned. Kavus
wants the sky and builds a throne to fly to heaven; Rostam wants the next mile of road — and only
one of them can keep a country. When the tyrant's snakes are feeding, it is not a speech about inner
dragons that ends it — it is **Kaveh's blacksmith apron** raised as a banner, an actual uprising,
the monster chained under a mountain. And Kay Khosrow, at the very end, does the most adult thing in
the whole epic: he wins the war he was born for — and then he **walks off the throne.** He completes
the myth. He does not become it.

That is the whole instruction, and it is where you now stand. **Love the myth. Take its lesson. Do
not move in.** Use the story in your head — then take the step, own the choice, and build the self
no fantasy can build for you. The myths were the mirror. What comes next is the work. *No one is born
with a fully developed soul. You have to work for that.*

## OPTION B — *The Two Snakes*

**Tagline:** Two heroes show you the two ways to lose your life inside a story. Then the harder
question: why does your mind keep building the story at all?

### Page 1 — The Snakes and the Name

Start with two men from the world you just left.

The first is Zahhak. The devil kisses his shoulders and two black snakes grow there. He hacks them
off; they grow back. The only thing that quiets them is to feed them — young men's brains, every
day, forever. Zahhak does not wake up one morning and *choose* to be a monster. **He agrees to a
feeding schedule,** and after that the appetite runs the palace and he only administers it.

The second is Rostam, the greatest champion alive — so welded to that name that when a young
warrior asks him, face to face, *are you Rostam?*, he lies to keep his edge. He does not know the
boy is his own son, Sohrab. He learns it from a token on the corpse, **after** the killing blow.

Hold them side by side, because they are the same warning twice. One man has moved into **his
wound.** The other has moved into **his legend.** And both of them, from inside the story they are
living, **eat their own future** — one a nation's sons, one his only child. *You cannot carry the
fire of the gods without burning down your own house.*

### Page 2 — Why You Build the Story

Now the strange part. You felt those tragedies. A snake-shouldered king and a champion who never
lived reached across a thousand years and *landed.* Why?

Because your mind is the same instrument that made them. It is a **myth-making machine.** Give it a
silence and it hears a voice; give it a shadow on Mars and it sees a face; give it a world too big
to hold and it hands you **a who** — a maker, a plan, a reason — because a universe run by persons
is easy to carry and a universe run by accident is not. Twenty-five centuries ago Xenophanes caught
the machine in the act: *if horses had hands, they would draw the gods like horses.* We always carve
the makers **in our own image.**

So the gods of Mythos were never out there. They came from in here — the same place your fear and
your hope and your reach come from. Which means the real subject was never the pantheon. **It was
the machine that keeps building pantheons,** the one behind your forehead, running right now. That
machine is what the rest of this series takes apart.

### Page 3 — Borrow the Sword, Then Leave

If the mind will build a myth whether you like it or not, the only question left is what you *do*
with the one it built. There are two answers, and Ferdowsi already staged both.

You can move in — like Zahhak, like Rostam — and let the story eat your life. Or you can treat the
myth the way Chesterton said a child treats a fairy tale: not as proof the dragon exists (you
already knew that), but as the promise that **the dragon can be killed** — a St. George to borrow,
and then carry back into your actual day. Tolkien called the wrong version desertion and the right
version recovery: you go into the story to return **stronger,** not to stay. When the snakes were
feeding a whole kingdom, what ended it was not a sermon about inner demons — it was **Kaveh's
blacksmith apron** on a spear, a real revolt, the tyrant chained under the mountain. And Kay
Khosrow, having won everything, did the one thing the lodgers never could: he **stepped off the
throne** and walked away. He finished the myth instead of becoming it.

That is the door you are standing in. **The myths are lessons, not lodging.** Keep the courage;
leave the castle. Take the step the story cannot take for you. *No one is born with a fully
developed soul. You have to work for that.*

## OPTION C — *A Self-Portrait*

**Tagline:** The gods never looked down at us. We looked into a mirror and called the reflection a
god. Once you see that, there is only one place left to look.

### Page 1 — The Face in the Mirror

Every god humanity has ever built has a face suspiciously like the people who built it. Bronze Age
gods quarrel over land and demand tribute — because that was the world of the people imagining them.
Warrior nations get warrior gods. Merchant cities get gods who keep accounts. The maker always turns
out to dress in the technology and the temper of its age.

Xenophanes said it first, and cleanest, twenty-five centuries ago: *if horses and oxen had hands,
they would draw the gods like horses and oxen.* Read that slowly, because it is not a joke about
horses. **It is about you.** The myths you just moved through were not dispatches from somewhere
else. They were a **self-portrait** — the human mind, painting its own fears and hungers on a scale
big enough to kneel before.

That is the quiet, vertiginous turn this whole series begins from. When you look up at the gods, the
gods are looking back with **your** eyes. Which means the door marked *out there* — heaven, fate,
the pantheon — was always, secretly, a door marked **in here.**

### Page 2 — The Comfortable Lie

The mind does not build these mirrors by accident; it is **built** to. It reads a who behind every
event, a plan behind every pattern, an intention behind the rustle in the grass — because for a
hundred thousand years the ancestor who guessed *predator* and was wrong lost nothing, and the one
who guessed *wind* and was wrong got eaten. So the machine runs hot. It hands you agents, stories,
destinies, all day, whether or not any are there.

And a story is a lovely place to live. Inside it you are the chosen one still awaiting the call, the
hero whose real life hasn't started. The Jungians call the person who settles there the **provisional
life** — everything *for now,* everything a rehearsal, the true thing always about to begin. Alan
Watts had the sharper image: such a person is *eating the menu instead of the meal.* You can starve
that way, he warned, **on a full description of a dinner you never ate.**

Your Mythos names the price. The champion Rostam is so lost in his own legend that the name becomes
a **map** he mistakes for the **territory** — and living inside the map, he kills the son standing in
front of him without once asking the boy's name. That is what the story does when you move in. It is
a beautiful place to disappear.

### Page 3 — Turn Around

So here is the whole of it, and then we begin.

The myth is not a house. It is a **finger pointing at the moon** — and if you stare at the finger you
lose both the moon and the finger. Its job was never to be lived in; it was to send you back to your
own life seeing it more clearly, with, as Tolkien put it, steadier courage. The fairy tale does not
tell you the dragon is real. It tells you the dragon can be **beaten** — and then it expects you to
go and do it. The hero's journey has a second half that the daydreamers always skip: **the return.**
Refuse it, said Campbell, and your flowering world *becomes a wasteland; your life feels meaningless.*

You have spent this long looking up at the makers. Now you know whose face they wore. There is only
one honest direction left to look, and it is the one the myths were quietly pointing at the whole
time: **inward, and then forward — into a life you actually live.** That turn is the first act of
agency, and agency is where the work begins. *No one is born with a fully developed soul. You have to
work for that.*
